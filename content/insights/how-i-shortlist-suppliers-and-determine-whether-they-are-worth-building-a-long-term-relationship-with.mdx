## Introduction

In today’s digital environment, finding suppliers through platforms such as 1688, TikTok, industry-specific marketplaces, and social media is no longer particularly difficult.

The real challenge is determining whether a supplier is genuinely suitable for your product, aligned with your company’s sourcing strategy, and worth the time and effort required to build a long-term working relationship.

If a supplier is not worth maintaining over the long term, they may still be able to complete the initial project, but you may eventually spend a significant amount of time dealing with communication issues, quality problems, delivery delays, and disputes over responsibility.

I would rather invest my time in suppliers who are worth developing, willing to cooperate, and capable of growing alongside the client. Building a stable relationship with the right suppliers creates a much stronger foundation for future projects.

For this reason, the focus of supplier management has gradually shifted from **“how to find suppliers”** to **“how to identify the suppliers that are genuinely right for the business.”**

Naturally, every company has different sourcing priorities.

Some companies are highly price-sensitive, while others are less concerned about achieving the lowest possible price and place greater importance on product quality, brand positioning, delivery reliability, and service capabilities.

Before evaluating suppliers, it is therefore essential to understand what the product requires and what the company values most. Only then can we determine whether a supplier is truly suitable for the project and aligned with the company’s long-term direction.

## Shortlisting Suppliers

Before approaching suppliers, I believe the most important step is not immediately requesting quotations. It is first developing a sufficient understanding of the product itself.

**The following points should be clarified in advance:**

* What are the product’s core requirements?
* What are the main manufacturing challenges?
* Are the proposed processes and structural designs technically achievable?
* Which stages are most likely to create problems during development or production?

Only when you understand the product yourself can you ask meaningful questions and determine whether the supplier’s answers are professional, practical, and technically reasonable.

Otherwise, even if a supplier provides an answer that sounds comprehensive, it may still be difficult to judge whether that answer is reliable or to identify the risks hidden behind it.

During my initial communication with a supplier, I usually focus on the following areas to determine whether they have the fundamental capabilities required to complete the project.

## 1. The Speed and Accuracy of Their Responses

* Response speed is not simply about how quickly a supplier replies to a message. More importantly, it is about whether they can understand the question quickly and provide a clear, accurate, and relevant answer.
* Some suppliers respond almost immediately, but their answers are vague and provide little practical value. Others may need time to verify the details internally, but they are able to provide complete technical information, production recommendations, or relevant project examples.
* Rather than focusing only on response time, I pay more attention to whether the information provided is accurate and whether it genuinely addresses the question I asked.

## 2. The Completeness of the Information They Provide

* A supplier who genuinely understands the product should usually be able to provide relevant product information, material specifications, process explanations, test reports, production examples, or quality standards based on the needs of the project.
* This information helps me determine not only whether they have produced similar products before, but also how effectively the supplier manages, records, and organizes information internally.

## 3. Whether They Proactively Guide the Project or Simply Answer Questions

* Throughout the communication process, I also observe whether the supplier actively helps guide the project or simply responds passively to the questions I raise.
* A genuinely experienced supplier will not simply wait for the client to ask questions. They will proactively seek to understand the product’s intended use, target market, order quantity, quality requirements, packaging method, and delivery schedule, and then provide recommendations based on their previous manufacturing experience.
* If a supplier can raise issues that I had not considered during the initial planning stage, it usually indicates that they have relevant experience and are capable of identifying manufacturing risks before those risks become actual problems.

> **A good supplier should not merely execute an order. They should also use their manufacturing knowledge to help the client reduce potential problems during product development and mass production.**

## 4. Reviewing Whether the Supplier Has Produced Similar Products

* After the initial communication, I will also investigate whether the supplier has previously manufactured the same or similar types of products.
* Even when their previous products are not identical to the product I plan to develop, I can still evaluate whether the processes, materials, and manufacturing experience involved are transferable to my project.

## 5. Conducting an On-Site Factory Visit Whenever Conditions Allow

* Online communication and supplier-provided materials can always be carefully prepared or presented in a favorable way. Once you visit the factory in person, however, many aspects of the actual operation become much more difficult to hide.
* Another important value of an on-site visit is the opportunity to ask questions directly to the factory owner, project manager, engineers, or production staff. Their answers can help verify whether the factory’s claimed manufacturing capabilities are genuine.
* In addition, visiting the factory can significantly strengthen mutual trust and gives both parties an opportunity to demonstrate how seriously they regard the potential cooperation.
* My willingness to visit the factory demonstrates that I take both the project and the potential relationship seriously. Similarly, if the factory is willing to host the visit, cooperate openly, and answer questions carefully, it shows that they are prepared to invest time in the project and approach the cooperation with sincerity.

## Evaluating Long-Term Fit

One aspect of my approach that may differ from that of many other sourcing professionals is that, in addition to factory size, equipment, pricing, and certifications, I pay close attention to the people involved.

I observe whether they avoid problems when challenges arise, whether they take an active approach to resolving them, and whether they are willing to work with the client to find a solution when there is disagreement over responsibility.

Problems are unavoidable in product development and manufacturing.

What truly determines whether a supplier relationship can continue over the long term is usually not how the supplier performs when everything is going smoothly. It is how they respond when something goes wrong and what they do to move the issue toward a practical resolution.

Some large factories have strong manufacturing capabilities, but that does not necessarily mean they will give your project sufficient attention. When the order quantity is relatively small, it may be difficult for the project to receive enough internal resources or management support.

Some smaller factories may not have the same overall resources as a large manufacturer, but they may be more willing to communicate, cooperate, and make adjustments according to the specific needs of the project.

**In many situations, I consider this willingness to cooperate more important.**

Large factories usually operate under highly standardized and fixed procedures, which makes it difficult for them to change their internal rules for an individual project. By contrast, a small or medium-sized supplier that genuinely values the project may be able to provide more flexible and responsive support.

This does not mean that smaller factories are necessarily better than larger ones. The important point is to determine which type of supplier is the best fit for the product, the order volume, and the company’s preferred way of working.

## Conclusion

From my perspective, there is no such thing as a **“perfect supplier”** that is suitable for every product and every client.

Throughout the cooperation, my role is to identify and fill any gaps, keep communication and execution moving smoothly between the client and the supplier, and evaluate decisions from the perspective and quality expectations of the client’s end customer.

I also see myself as a dependable part of the overall supply-chain structure—stepping in wherever support is needed and making sure that no critical issue is left unattended.

I have always believed that supplier cooperation is ultimately built on relationships between people.

> **When you find the right people to work with, many product-development and manufacturing challenges are already halfway solved.**
